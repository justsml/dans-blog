# Working notes: Outsmart Your Lying, Cheating Students

Dictated 2026-09-05. Cleaned and grouped by theme. Each theme names the slide in the [40-minute outline](../../outlines/evidence-learning-40min.md) that now carries it. Open items are at the end.

## The new spine

The talk used to open on detectors asking the wrong question. It now opens on acceptance: the horse is out of the barn. The arc becomes embrace → foundations without AI → teach AI on purpose → measure and record → protect → pilot.

## 1. Open by embracing it (slide 1)

- The historical parallels are beaten to death, so use one and move on: the calculator panic, specifically the Casio calculator watch. The fear was that math class was dead, that the kid next to the Casio owner didn't even need one.
- Confession beat: "I may have used one or two on an assessment." Then the pivot: "But we're not here for that. What I did was wholesome. These kids are phoning it in. Not even trying. Infuriating, right?" The self-incrimination is the joke that makes the title land.
- The teacher ancestors clutching their calculators had to write a real trajectory into the curriculum. So do we.

## 2. The doom loop (slide 2)

- The defeat is real. Proctoring and assessment tools fail readily. The only thing that works is a pop quiz or a cold call, and it proves the student can't hit the first level of Bloom's taxonomy. Can't recall it, can't spell it.
- That crushes the educator and cements a negative feedback loop: the student learns they can cheat their way through, and the reward is getting away with it. Even when caught, there is little stigma for using a tool as common as a phone.
- Pummeling students with pop quizzes and gotcha questions only proves what you already know: Johnny ain't learning too good.
- Lockdowns fail. Block ChatGPT on the network and they share a VPN from a phone. Students will find a way. They are the original adversarial actors. (Jurassic Park line: life finds a way.)

## 3. Foundations first, stated in the curriculum (slide 3)

- Say plainly in the curriculum where pencil, paper, and whiteboard live, and why: the spatial and motor dimensions activated by a handwriting instrument, talking problems over with peers, finding a cohort.
- Craft the environment where core foundational steps still happen. Then tell students: we are going to show you how to use all of this.

## 4. Make verification feel like cheating (new slide 5)

- The high-leverage assignment: photograph a sheet of a hundred arithmetic problems and make the LLM do them. Slip in a trick: a 1 that looks like a 7, a smudge that turns an 8 into a 9.
- The moment: "How'd you get the wrong answers? You were supposed to scan it in. Oh, you didn't check the results? Johnny, you're not gonna make it."
- That experience teaches verification better than any ban, filter, or lockdown.
- Hand out the prompt like a worksheet: "paste these instructions as your first or system message." Expectation: record the screen or export the whole session.
- Higher-level students get freedom to experiment as a meta learning objective: the writing challenge, the rhyming challenge, whatever riddles English teachers love. The constraint that keeps it honest: personalize it. Narrate from summer camp, reference this week's unit, meet a criterion the model can't know.
- Privacy teaching moment inside that assignment: if you share Lake Tahoe, maybe don't share the photo of your grandparents' place on the water that any model can geolocate. Nobody is teaching students how these systems work mechanically.

## 5. Measure AI time like talk time (new slide 9)

- Teachers already track teacher talk time, student talk time, silence and think time. Add a factor: AI time. How much is guided by the educator, how much is individual student interaction.
- Now imagine seeing across 30 or 300 students how they got to the answer. Take every transcript and run a qualitative analysis with the LLM: show me five examples where students led a novel line of inquiry, or reached the right answer by a notable path.
- The same pass surfaces the students who happened to type exactly the right terms, or pasted a large block of text they clearly copied from somewhere. Caveat: big blocks also look like voice-to-text input. Watch for that before concluding anything.

## 6. Voice: tells, social pressure, and sensitivity (new slide 10)

- Speaking reduces the ability to cheat. Answering from recall and reading an LLM's output aloud produce different classes of errors. Readers struggle to keep up with text wrapping and jumping; they mispronounce words they would never have chosen from memory. Spoken language carries more tells, enough to categorize likelihood, never to convict.
- Small groups in class, speaking, resets the table. It adds social pressure against cheating and hands the educator knobs on the incentive structure that today is a magical cheating genie.
- Not foolproof. A student can build an agent told to "seem dumb at first, then learn." Hard to make anything totally foolproof. That is fine; the goal is changing the incentives, not certainty.
- Voice fingerprinting of students is plausible technology. Treat that as a reason for care, not a feature. Student voices are highly sensitive, identify them, and can be cloned. Any student-facing voice application gets the utmost care, a strong preference for maximum privacy and lowest risk, and age-appropriate use only.
- Newer offline models detect frustration and distress in voice with high confidence. Offline matters: it can meet zero-data-retention and no-sharing rules in many districts on modest hardware. Same care applies.

## 7. Write the objective to the agent (new slide 12)

- Observed in the wild: students tell a voice assistant "you're supposed to tutor me, don't give me answers, I'm here with three classmates, we're talking about Lincoln." Give it the learning objective by having students photograph the whiteboard.
- Prediction: we may end up writing objectives on the board addressed to the agents. "Tutor bot, help my students with..." The agent then ensures and assesses to the highest rigor possible, explores stretch goals, fills remedial gaps, and shares the data with the teacher, aides, and parents as needed.
- Pre-programmed models can recommend learning opportunities, probe for weaknesses, hold a working memory of a learning path, and follow your preferred objective-writing process (the 4Ms, or another program; see open items).
- Study discipline: proactively push "want a practice run? flashcards? a pop quiz?" at first, then reward students who ask for it unprompted, before the test.
- Reinforce the connection distracted students miss: "five flashcards an hour before produced a 98, a 30% improvement over your last three tests with no prep." (Illustrative numbers, not data.)
- Personalized recaps as short podcasts or MP3s for the drive to school. Adapt to the student's life: after band practice is not the time for complex chemistry; a free period late afternoon produces better results.
- Personal angle: as someone with ADHD, a personal assistant at my fingertips or voice that reminds me, reorders my day, and notices "you did badly on the section quizzes after that reading, take a 20-minute walk" is not a luxury. Conversational agents can apply cognitive-science best practice to balance time, avoid cramming, and support wellbeing.

## 8. Widen the door (new slide 14)

- Memory: math class, art class, wildly varying enthusiasm. Told I was terrible at art; in my case it turned out true. For many it was disbelief in themselves or the cost of supplies beyond standard issue.
- The sculptor: a friend who discovered after school they were a talented sculptor, later won awards, and once lamented never touching the wet clay in class. Parents had no car. "I'm not carrying a brick of clay in my bag." That was it.
- Those barriers reflect our socioeconomic structure and sit unexamined in the system. Models can expand who participates: "I'm no good with a pencil on graph paper, but I like that look." Make the charcoal graph-paper sketch. Animate the birds at the lunch tables. Then it's a short jump: make it 3D, make it a game asset. Ratchet up, lock in, stretch. Out of ideas? Ask the model.

## 9. Close: a purpose-built machine (slide 16)

- This is a great deal of data and a great deal of power. Too much to hold casually. The value has to be proven constantly, for educators and students, or the data should not exist.
- No ambling through casual conversations dispensing answers. Purpose-built. There are technical ways to achieve this; come talk to me after.
- Teaching has to change: how we include agents in the classroom, model their use, and design group efforts around them.

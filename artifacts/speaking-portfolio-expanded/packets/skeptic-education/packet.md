## Abstracts

### 50 words

Neither "every student gets a private genius" nor "every assignment is now worthless" tells you what to do on Monday. This talk gives educators a method: name the capability, decide where assistance belongs, anticipate the four ways AI fails in a course, and earn confidence through one small pilot instead of a policy war.

### 100 words

Skepticism is a method, not a mood. This talk assumes AI access will persist and asks what an educator can do with justified confidence. It offers the Delegate, Augment, Preserve framework for placing assistance relative to a learning objective, then walks through four failure modes to anticipate: the tutor that agrees with a wrong answer, assisted gains that vanish on the unaided test, the detector used as judge, and unequal access. Each comes with evidence from recent trials and a handling. A pre-mortem exercise and a one-assignment pilot plan close the session. Delivered by an engineer who has built AI systems for education.

### 250 words

Educators hear two promises: a private genius for every student, or every assignment worthless. Neither tells you what to do on Monday. This talk starts from a planning assumption, that AI access will remain available enough that curriculum cannot depend on preventing it, and builds a method for adopting it with justified confidence.

The method has four moves. Name the capability an assignment develops, separate from the artifact it produces. Decide where assistance belongs using Delegate, Augment, Preserve: the same help lands in different columns as the objective changes, and a short demo shows that shift. Anticipate the four failure modes: a tutor that affirms a plausible wrong answer; assisted practice that improves while unaided performance falls, as in the Bastani high-school mathematics trial; a probabilistic detector treated as a judge, with Liang's findings on nonnative writers; and access policies that grade a student's tools instead of their thinking. Handle each with a local evaluation set, proportional evidence, and a human at consequential decisions.

Evidence stays attached to its setting. The Kestin physics trial shows a designed tutor can outperform active learning on immediate outcomes; together the studies argue for attention to instructional design rather than a verdict on AI.

The session peaks with a pre-mortem: it is week ten, the policy failed, what happened? It lands on a pilot of one assignment with baseline evidence, estimated staff time, and calibrated graders, plus the questions to ask a vendor. The worked example is a history essay weighing two conflicting sources.

## Learning outcomes

Attendees will be able to:

1. Place assistance for a specific assignment into Delegate, Augment, or Preserve and state the learning objective that justifies the placement.
2. Name the four failure modes and match each with a concrete handling they can apply in one course.
3. Design a one-assignment pilot with baseline evidence, a simple rubric, estimated staff time, and a criterion for continuing.

## Audience and prerequisites

Instructors, instructional designers, academic technologists, and program leads in secondary and higher education. No technical background required. Bring one real assignment.

## Practical takeaways

- The Delegate, Augment, Preserve worksheet with the "because students need to learn" prompt.
- A four-case local evaluation set to run against any tutor before a pilot.
- The vendor questions: what learning improved, compared with what, for whom, at what staff cost.

## Not a product pitch

The speaker has built AI systems at an education technology company. The talk recommends no product, names no vendor's tool, and includes a slide on how to interrogate any vendor's claims. The demo is offline and vendor-free.

## Audience-specific abstracts (100 words each)

### Engineering practitioner

Engineers building education products inherit a debate they did not start. This session gives the technical audience the educator's view: what a learning objective is, why a polished artifact is not evidence of it, and the four ways AI tools fail in a course, sycophancy, vanished gains, detector-as-judge, and unequal access. Each failure maps to something you can build or test: a local eval set with a confident wrong answer, separate logging of supported and independent performance, and no automated consequence from a probability score. You leave knowing what an educator will ask of your product and how to answer.

### Engineering leadership and product

Education products live or die on a question most roadmaps skip: did the learner get better without the tool? This talk, for product and engineering leaders in edtech, explains the Delegate, Augment, Preserve framework educators use to place assistance, walks through recent trials where assisted gains vanished or held depending on tutor design, and shows the four failure modes a pilot must anticipate. It closes with the procurement questions buyers are learning to ask and how a product team can answer them honestly. The speaker has built AI systems at an education technology company and names no product.

### Education and instructional design

Skepticism is a method, not a mood. Assuming students will have AI access, what can an instructor do with justified confidence? This session offers the Delegate, Augment, Preserve framework for placing assistance against a learning objective, four failure modes to anticipate with evidence from recent trials, a pre-mortem exercise for one assignment, and a pilot plan with baseline evidence and calibrated grading. The worked example is a history essay weighing conflicting sources. Bring one real assignment; leave with its capability named, its assistance placed, and its evidence chosen. No technical background needed.

### Executive and general technology

Institutions are being sold two stories: AI transforms learning, or AI destroys it. This talk gives leaders a third position, a method for adopting with evidence. It explains why a polished artifact is not proof of learning, how the same AI help can be appropriate or harmful depending on the objective, and what recent trials found when assistance was measured against later unaided performance. It ends with the questions to ask any vendor and a pilot small enough to run next term. No technical background required. The speaker has built AI systems for education and recommends no product.

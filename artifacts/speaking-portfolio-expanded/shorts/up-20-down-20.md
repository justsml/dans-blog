# Up 20, Down 20, Out Four Bucks

2 min · video · parent: [Outsmart Your Lying, Cheating Students](../outlines/evidence-learning-40min.md), slides 4 and 7

Clean prose, perfect confidence, wrong base. A final-answer grader catches this exactly never.

## Hook

A shop raises a $100 price by 20%, then discounts the new price by 20%. The student writes: "Equal and opposite, so the final price is $100." Great sentence. Wrong.

## Beat: the base moved

$100 → $120 → $96. The second percentage acts on the new base. Now imagine the student wrote $96 with that same faulty explanation hiding behind it, because a model gave them the number. What would a final-answer assessment have told you? Nothing. The capability you want is narrow: explain why a percentage acts on a base, and how the base changes across a sequence.

## Beat: the transfer item

Change the numbers: $80, up 25%, down 20%. It comes back to $80, because 1.25 × 0.8 = 1. That breaks the shortcut the room just formed, "opposite changes never cancel," which is the point. Same invariant, different numbers is where you find out whether they learned the rule or memorized the example.

## Landing

Score the reasoning: base identified, calculation justified, transfer explained. Put help in its own column. A right number with no explanation is a one, not a two.

## On screen

`$100 → $120 → $96`. Then `$80 → $100 → $80`. Caption: *why does the second base change?*

## Source

Soderstrom and Bjork (2015), Learning versus performance, on why supported performance and later unaided performance come apart.

# Till Renewal Do Us Part

6 min · lightning · parent: [Cry Me a Free Tier](../outlines/free-tier-40min.md), slides 12 and 13

The customer-has-the-power framing is wrong. Your counterparty has more money than you, and your architecture is the collateral.

## Hook (0:00)

Everyone treats picking a model provider like shopping: the customer holds the power because they can leave. Oliver Williamson won a Nobel for explaining why that stops being true the moment you invest in the relationship.

## Beat: asset specificity (1:00)

An investment worth a great deal inside this relationship and very little outside it. That surplus, the part that exists only because you're locked in, is a prize, and at renewal your counterparty can reach for it. Nobody has to be a villain. It's what the structure permits, and rational parties do it. The word for the reaching is hold-up.

## Beat: inventory yours (2:00)

Prompts tuned against one model's quirks. An eval suite that only means anything against one provider's outputs. A fine-tune. A latency budget that assumes one serving stack. And the sharpest one: an unlimited-usage promise you made to your customers, priced against an input you don't control and can't cap. Every one of those is worth more here than anywhere else.

## Beat: reversibility is an option, and options have a premium (3:15)

"Keep it reversible" isn't a personality trait; it's a real option in Dixit and Pindyck's sense. Its value rises with price uncertainty and your exposure. Its premium is the abstraction tax, a second eval suite, rehearsal time, a generic wrapper that hides real differences between models. Don't argue portability on vibes. Exposure is three prices times your volume. Premium is the engineering. Compare the numbers.

## Landing (5:00)

Stop thinking of this as shopping. You're contracting with a much better capitalized counterparty. Reduce specificity where it's cheap, price the exposure where it isn't, and rehearse the divorce once before you need it.

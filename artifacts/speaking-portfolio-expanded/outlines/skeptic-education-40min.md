# A Skeptic's Guide to Surviving AI in Education

40 minutes · 14 slides

**In one line.** How to adopt AI in a course with justified confidence: name the capability, decide where assistance belongs, anticipate the four ways it fails, and handle each with a small pilot instead of a policy war.

**Arc.** Warm open, steady through the framework, build through the demo and the four failure modes, peak at the pre-mortem, land on the pilot.

**Scope.** Planning assumption: AI access will remain available enough that curriculum cannot depend on preventing it. Research stays attached to its setting. The framework exercise and the demo are design tools. Say it once on slide 1.

**Demo.** [Runbook section 1](../demos/DEMO-RUNBOOK.md#1-a-skeptics-guide-to-surviving-ai-in-education) · [Kit](../demos/index.html). Fallback: narrate the three cases on slide 5.

**Worked example.** A history essay weighing two conflicting primary sources. The percentage-change fixture belongs to the assessment-design talk, so the two can be booked together.

**Before each delivery.** Fill the `Story` lines.

**Image style.** Dark slate background, one amber accent, flat vector, generous negative space, no text or logos. Each slide comment is a complete prompt.

**Timings** are rehearsal targets, no Q&A. Notes are cues.

## 1. Assume access. Demand evidence.

0:00–2:30 · warm

<!-- image: a classroom desk with an open notebook, a glowing phone face down beside it, a window with morning light, dark slate background, amber accent on the phone edge, flat vector, no text -->

> Preserve the reason students are here.

Educators hear two promises: every student gets a private genius, or every assignment is now worthless. Neither tells you what to do on Monday.

Scope, once: I assume access will persist, so we design around it. Research today stays attached to its setting. The exercise is a design tool. From here on, recommendations.

Hold one real assignment in mind. By the end you should be able to name its capability, place assistance, and choose evidence.

Story: [the assignment of yours or a colleague's that AI broke first, and what you did that week]

## 2. Skepticism is a method, not a mood

2:30–5:00 · warm

<!-- image: a magnifying glass held over a glossy brochure, the lens revealing plain handwritten notes underneath, dark slate background, amber accent on the lens rim, flat vector, no text -->

> What is the claim?
> What would count as evidence?
> Who benefits if we believe it?

Skepticism should produce better questions, not permanent refusal. A vendor says the tutor improves learning: ask what students did unaided afterward. A colleague says the tool destroys thinking: ask which task, which learners, which help. Both need a comparison. Write the claim so it could fail: "students using this hint sequence will explain source bias on a new document next week."

Two failures you will hear about this year: the confident wrong answer that got an A, and the pilot that improved scores and nothing else. Both are design failures, and both can be anticipated.

## 3. The proxy is not the capability

5:00–7:00 · steady

<!-- image: a beautifully bound essay on a pedestal casting a shadow, the shadow shaped like a question mark, dark slate background, amber accent on the shadow, flat vector, no text -->

> Artifact: a polished essay
> Capability: weigh conflicting sources
> Evidence: explain a choice and revise it

A formatted essay proves an essay exists. Our objective: given two primary sources that disagree about the same event, explain which to trust more for a specific question and why. A polished essay can summarize both and never make the judgment. Keep the artifact; add the moment where the reasoning is visible.

## 4. Delegate / Augment / Preserve

7:00–10:00 · steady

<!-- image: three vertical columns like sorting bins, a hand dropping a single verb-shaped token into the middle one, dark slate background, amber accent on the token, flat vector, no text -->

> Delegate effort outside the objective
> Augment practice toward the objective
> Preserve evidence of the objective

Decisions about a learner on a task at a stage, not permanent lists. Formatting citations: delegate in a seminar, preserve when citation is the lesson. Suggesting a counter-reading: augment during drafting, preserve during the independent check. The same activity moves columns when the objective changes.

Write it (60 s): one verb from your assignment in a column, and finish "because students need to learn [blank]." If the reason is missing, the tool policy is doing the curriculum's job badly.

## 5. Demo: same assistance, three verdicts

10:00–14:30 · build

<!-- image: one identical helping hand icon shown three times, each time landing in a different one of three bins, dark slate background, amber accent on the third bin, flat vector, no text -->

> Spelling without assistance → Preserve
> Strength of an argument → Augment
> Scientific explanation → Delegate

Follow [runbook section 1](../demos/DEMO-RUNBOOK.md#1-a-skeptics-guide-to-surviving-ai-in-education). Same general assistance; the verdict changes with the capability under assessment. Translate to the history example: grammar help is Delegate; a counter-reading is Augment while drafting; the final "which source and why" paragraph is Preserve.

Ask (45 s): for your assignment, what evidence would show the capability if the artifact were produced with help?

Compression: at two minutes, one switch, state the other two.

## 6. Failure mode 1: the tutor that agrees with you

14:30–17:00 · build

<!-- image: a chat bubble with a thumbs-up emoji next to a chat bubble containing a question mark, the thumbs-up bubble slightly cracked, dark slate background, amber accent on the crack, flat vector, no text -->

> Student: "The diary is more reliable because it is older."
> Tutor A: "Great point!"
> Tutor B: "Older than what? What would make a diary unreliable?"

Sycophancy is the first failure to expect. A tutor that affirms a plausible mistake trains the mistake. Handling is on slide 11: confident wrong answers go into your local eval set before any pilot.

Story: [a confidently wrong AI answer you or a student accepted]

## 7. Failure mode 2: gains that vanish, and when they don't

17:00–21:00 · build

<!-- image: two line graphs side by side, the first rising then dropping off a cliff, the second rising and holding, a small scaffold drawn under the second line, dark slate background, amber accent on the scaffold, flat vector, no text -->

> Bastani: assisted practice up, unaided performance down, safeguards helped
> Kestin: a designed tutor beat active learning on immediate outcomes
> Which learners, which teaching moves, which comparison, how long after

Bastani and colleagues ran a high-school mathematics trial. Unrestricted GPT access improved practice and worsened later unaided performance; a version with teacher-designed safeguards mitigated the harm. One intervention, one setting. Reuse the design lesson, not a headline percentage, and check the corrected article before quoting numbers.

Kestin and colleagues found a carefully engineered AI tutor outperformed the active-learning comparison in a university physics course on immediate outcomes. Keep the treatment attached to the finding.

Together the two studies should make you curious about instructional design rather than eager to pick a winner. Ask what the tutor makes the learner do: attempt, expose an assumption, check a new example. Handling: record supported and independent performance separately. A dashboard counting completed exercises tells you the system is busy.

Sources: Bastani et al. (2025), [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), PNAS, with a [correction](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122) · Kestin et al. (2025), [AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6), Scientific Reports.

## 8. Failure mode 3: the detector as judge

21:00–23:00 · build

<!-- image: a courtroom gavel resting on a probability dial reading somewhere in the middle, the dial needle trembling, dark slate background, amber accent on the needle, flat vector, no text -->

> Authorship inference ≠ learning evidence ≠ misconduct decision

Liang and colleagues found the detectors they tested produced false positives on nonnative English writing and were sensitive to rewriting. Evidence about tested tools, not every future detector. Even a good authorship signal would not tell you whether the student can weigh sources. Handling: no disciplinary consequence is automated from a score. And no detector stunt on stage.

Source: Liang et al. (2023), [GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779), Patterns.

## 9. Failure mode 4: unequal access

23:00–25:00 · build

<!-- image: two identical doors, one with a wide ramp and one with a tall step, dark slate background, amber accent on the ramp, flat vector, no text -->

> Comparable tools or a usable alternative
> Accessible ways to explain
> Clear permission before the task; no surprise surveillance

If assisted practice is permitted, provide comparable access. If oral explanation is chosen, plan accommodations and language needs so you are not grading speaking ease by accident. State what is retained and who sees it. A policy that creates unequal access distorts the measurement before the first answer.

## 10. Pre-mortem: it is week ten and the policy failed

25:00–29:00 · peak

<!-- image: a calendar page torn off showing a distant week, a small crack running across the page, two chairs facing each other beneath it, dark slate background, amber accent on the crack, flat vector, no text -->

> What happened?
> What is the smallest change that would have caught it?

Pairs, two minutes. One partner names how the assignment's AI policy failed: confirmed error, vanished gains, false accusation, unequal access, or something new. The other names the smallest change that would have caught it. Then thirty seconds of shared answers.

This is how confidence is earned. Not by trusting the tool, but by having already imagined its failure and put a check there.

## 11. Handle it: a local eval set and proportional evidence

29:00–32:30 · steady

<!-- image: four index cards fanned out on a table, one marked with an X, one with a checkmark and a question mark, one with a hand asking, one with a tangled line, dark slate background, amber accent on the X card, flat vector, no text -->

> Eval set: a confident wrong answer, a right answer with faulty reasoning, a request for the solution, a learner the tutor misunderstands
> Evidence: one added question, a changed constraint, a correction with a reason, a later independent check

Four cases before any pilot. For the history example, the tutor must not affirm "older means more reliable." Two educators score the teaching moves; factual errors counted separately from pacing and tone. A benchmark score cannot certify your lesson.

Then proportional evidence. Add one question that improves the inference: "which source did you trust more, and what would change your mind?" Collect enough to support a decision, not enough to reconstruct a student's life.

## 12. Humans belong at consequential decisions

32:30–34:30 · steady

<!-- image: a long conveyor of identical worksheets with one worksheet lifted out and held in a pair of hands, dark slate background, amber accent on the lifted sheet, flat vector, no text -->

> Diagnose the misconception · Interpret ambiguous evidence · Own consequential decisions

AI scales instruction far more easily than it scales wisdom. A tutor supplies another exercise fast. A teacher notices the student reasons well aloud but freezes on the written form, or that a language issue is being scored as a history error. Reserve human attention for interpretation and consequential grading. A human escalation promise needs an actual route.

## 13. Pilot one assignment; ask one better question

34:30–38:00 · land

<!-- image: a single seedling in a small pot on a windowsill beside a large empty planter, dark slate background, amber accent on the seedling, flat vector, no text -->

> Name the capability · Specify permitted assistance
> Compare evidence before and after · Decide whether to keep it
> To the vendor: what learning improved, compared with what, for whom, at what staff cost?

Redesign one assignment, not the institution. Write the objective and a simple rubric. Keep a baseline sample. Estimate staff time. Agree what improvement justifies continuing. Invite students to report confusing instructions. Two educators grade a sample and resolve disagreements first. TEQSA's guidance supports multiple contextual approaches; this is one local implementation.

In the next vendor demo, ask them to walk through one learner misconception and show the teaching response. Ask what was measured after assistance stopped, what happens when the tutor is wrong, and what work falls back onto staff. A tool that saves preparation time without improving learning may still be worth buying if it is sold that way.

Story: [a pilot you ran or watched: what surprised you, what you kept]

Source: TEQSA (2023), [Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligence](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence).

## 14. Keep the reason students are here

38:00–40:00 · land

<!-- image: the classroom desk from slide 1, the phone now face up showing a blank screen, the notebook filled with three handwritten lines, dark slate background, amber accent on the notebook lines, flat vector, no text -->

> Name the capability. Choose assistance deliberately.
> Collect evidence of understanding. Evaluate the teaching, not the hype.

Write it (45 s): the learner should be able to [blank]; assistance is useful at [blank]; I will know they can do it when [blank].

The goal is not education where students don't use AI. It is education where using AI doesn't eliminate the reason they're there. Change one prompt, add one check, look at what the evidence lets you conclude.

# Outsmart Your Lying, Cheating Students

40 minutes · 16 slides

**Event-friendly title.** Stop Trying to Catch Students Using AI. Offer both; the title slide carries whichever the organizer chose.

**In one line.** The cat is out of the bag. Stop trying to catch students, state where the foundations happen without AI, teach the tool on purpose, and redesign one assessment so the question becomes "what does the learner understand?" instead of "who typed the sentence?"

**Arc.** Warm open on the confession, warm through the doom loop, steady through foundations and fixture, build at the smudge assignment, peak at the live reconstruction, steady through rubric and record, build through AI time, voice, and the agent-addressed objective, warm at the sculptor, peak at the worksheet, land on the purpose-built machine.

**Scope.** Fixtures are authored for the demonstration. The rubric is proposed, not validated. The reconstruction shows evidence collection, not a learning effect. Voice tells categorize; they never convict. Integrity processes stay in place. Say it once on slide 1.

## 1. The cat is out of the bag

0:00–2:30 · warm

<!-- image: a vintage calculator wristwatch resting on an open math worksheet, the watch face glowing faintly, a pencil laid across the page, dark slate background, amber accent on the watch face, flat vector, no text -->

> Horse out of the barn. Cat out of the bag.
> Math class survived the Casio watch.

The historical parallels have been beaten to death, so one and done: the calculator panic, specifically the Casio calculator watch. Math class was dead. The kid sitting next to the Casio owner didn't even need one.

Confession: I may have used one or two on an assessment. But we're not here for that. What I did was wholesome. These kids are phoning it in. Not even trying. Infuriating, right?

That laugh is the title doing its job. The provocation points at a brittle measurement strategy, not at students. Our calculator-clutching ancestors had to write a real trajectory into the curriculum. So do we.

Scope, once: authored fixtures, a proposed rubric, an exercise that shows evidence collection. Integrity processes stay. From here: what we stop doing, what we protect, what we teach, what we measure.

Story: [the submission that made you realize the grade and the understanding had come apart]

## 2. The doom loop

2:30–5:00 · warm

<!-- image: a figure at a locked gate holding a clipboard, behind the gate a small open window with a phone propped in it and a rope ladder hanging down, dark slate background, amber accent on the phone, flat vector, no text -->

> Proctoring fails. The pop quiz proves what you already knew.
> Getting away with it is the reward. Lockdowns are a puzzle, not a wall.
> Tested tool ≠ every detector · Authorship ≠ understanding

It can feel like you've lost. The proctoring and assessment tools fail readily. What still works is the cold call or the pop quiz, and it proves the student can't hit the first rung of Bloom's taxonomy. Can't recall it. Can't spell it. That is crushing, and it cements a negative feedback loop: the student learns they can cheat through, and getting away with it is the reward. Even when caught, there is little stigma for using something as common as a phone.

Pummeling students with gotcha quizzes only proves what you already know: Johnny ain't learning too good. Block the chatbot on the network and they share a VPN from a phone. Students will find a way. They are the original adversarial actors.

The detector is the same trap in software. Liang and colleagues found fairness and robustness problems in the detectors they tested; cite the narrow finding. Even with authorship settled, the second question remains: can the learner do the task? Never outsource an accusation to a probabilistic score, and never mistake a clean score for learning.

Source: Liang et al. (2023), [GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779), Patterns.

## 3. Say where the foundations happen, then teach the tool

5:00–7:30 · steady

<!-- image: a whiteboard with a hand-drawn diagram on the left half and a laptop propped on the right half, a marker resting in the tray between them, dark slate background, amber accent on the marker, flat vector, no text -->

> Pencil, paper, whiteboard, peers: named in the curriculum, with the reason
> Then: "we are going to show you how to use all of this"
> Capability: explain sequential percentage changes, identify the denominator, repair, transfer

Write the trajectory down. Where does handwriting live, and why: the spatial and motor work a pencil activates, the argument you have out loud with a peer, the cohort you find by talking through a problem. Craft the environment where those foundations still happen. Then tell students, in the same document, that you will teach them the tool.

For the rest of this talk, the capability is narrow enough that the room can inspect the evidence: the learner must explain why percentages act on a base and how the base changes across a sequence. Independent competence and competent tool use both belong in a course. They need different evidence. Say which one each checkpoint measures.

## 5. Make verification feel like cheating

9:00–12:00 · build

<!-- image: a phone photographing a worksheet dense with small arithmetic problems, one digit on the sheet smudged, a magnifying glass lying beside the phone, dark slate background, amber accent on the smudge, flat vector, no text -->

> "Take a picture of these hundred problems and make the model do them."
> A 1 that looks like a 7. A smudge that makes an 8 a 9.
> "You didn't check the results? Johnny, you're not gonna make it."

The high-leverage assignment lets students feel like they're cheating. Photograph the sheet, feed it to the model, submit. The trap is in the sheet: a stroke that reads as either digit, a smudge. The wrong answers come back and the lesson teaches itself. You were supposed to check.

That experience beats a ban, a filter, or a lockdown. Hand the prompt out like a worksheet: "paste these instructions as your first message." Expect a recorded screen or an exported session. Older students get room to experiment as the learning objective itself: the writing challenge, the rhyming challenge, whatever riddles the English teachers love. The constraint that keeps it honest is personalization. Narrate from summer camp. Reference this week's unit. Meet a criterion the model cannot know.

Privacy rides along. If you share Lake Tahoe, maybe not the photo of your grandparents' place on the water that any model can geolocate. Nobody is teaching students how these systems work mechanically. This assignment does.

Ask (30 s): what would the smudge be in your subject?

## 6. Ask for an attempt, then escalate help deliberately

12:00–14:30 · steady

<!-- image: a staircase of five shallow steps with a small figure on the second step, a hand offering a single step-sized block from the side, a speech bubble shaped like a key above the first step, dark slate background, amber accent on the offered block, flat vector, no text -->

> "Which price does the discount use?" Learner: "The original $100."
> Clarify → conceptual hint → missing step → part of an example → full explanation → fresh reconstruction

Two scripted turns. The diagnostic question reveals where the base was lost. The tutor neither praises the error nor dumps a solution. Attempt-first must not punish a student who cannot begin; clarifying terms or a parallel example may be the right move. Instructions express intent; they do not guarantee a model follows it.

Then the ladder. Clarification: the second change acts on the new price. Conceptual hint: identify the current base. Missing step: the intermediate $120. Partial example: multiply a different amount by 0.8. Full explanation, then a fresh reconstruction later. Record help because supported and independent performance mean different things. Never as a moral failure.

## 7. Demo: the four-minute reconstruction

14:30–19:30 · peak

<!-- image: three price tags in a row reading as shapes only, the middle one larger, an arrow looping from the third back to the first, dark slate background, amber accent on the looping arrow, flat vector, no text -->

> $100 → $120 → $96. Why does the second base change?
> Now: $80 → +25% → −20%

Follow [runbook section 5](../demos/DEMO-RUNBOOK.md#5-outsmart-your-lying-cheating-students) for the two scripted learners. Then live: reveal the wrong argument, let the room find the base, reveal $120 only after the diagnostic question, ask for $96 and a sentence.

Change to $80, +25%, −20%. It returns to $80 because 1.25 × 0.8 = 1. That breaks the shortcut "opposite changes never cancel."

If the room is quiet, present the prepared learner response. Never pressure an individual into a public assessment.

Compression: at two minutes, skip the kit, run only the $100 sequence, state the $80 item.

## 8. Score the reasoning; store observations, not a label

19:30–22:00 · steady

<!-- image: a three-row scorecard with small filled and empty circles beside a filing card with four short lines and a pencil eraser at the corner, a heavy metal stamp lying unused on its side, dark slate background, amber accent on the eraser, flat vector, no text -->

> Base identified 0–2 · Calculation justified 0–2 · Transfer explained 0–2 · Assistance recorded separately
> Record: concept · observed explanation · support provided · next check and review date

Zero: the wrong base persists. One: the right amount, no explanation. Two: the learner explains the current amount. Assistance goes in its own column so educators can interpret support. No deduction for asking a question. Calibrate on sample work before consequential use.

The record: concept, changing percentage base; observed, second percentage used the original amount; support, conceptual hint; next check, new item after practice. Never "weak at reasoning" or "likely cheated." Separate what the learner said from what a model inferred. Follow-ups follow a blueprint, not chatbot curiosity: "does reversing the multipliers change the result?" probes structure; a harder follow-up is not a fair comparison. A teacher should be able to see why each question was asked.

## 9. Measure AI time the way you measure talk time

22:00–24:30 · build

<!-- image: a classroom clock face divided into four unequal wedges, three wedges plain and one wedge patterned with tiny chat bubbles, dark slate background, amber accent on the patterned wedge, flat vector, no text -->

> Teacher talk · student talk · think time · AI time
> Guided by the educator, or individual?
> Across 30 or 300 transcripts: "show me five students who took a notable path"

Teachers already count who is talking, how much silence there is, whose engagement it is. Add one factor: AI time. How much of it does the educator guide, and how much is a student alone with the model.

Then the view you have never had. Every transcript from a class of 30 or a course of 300, and a qualitative pass run by the model: the five students who opened a novel line of inquiry, the ones who reached the right answer by a path worth showing the room. The same pass surfaces the student who typed exactly the right terms, or pasted a large block of text from somewhere else. Caveat before any conclusion: a big block also looks like voice-to-text. Look first.

Story: [a transcript that showed you a path to the answer you would not have taught]

## 10. Voice has tells; treat it with care

24:30–27:00 · build

<!-- image: two sound waveforms side by side, the first smooth and rounded, the second jagged with small breaks where lines wrap, a small padlock icon beneath both, dark slate background, amber accent on the padlock, flat vector, no text -->

> Recall errors and reading errors are different classes
> Small groups, out loud: the incentives reset
> Voice is biometric. Offline models. Zero retention. Age-appropriate only.

Answering from memory and reading a model's output aloud produce different mistakes. The reader falls behind as text wraps and jumps; they mispronounce a word they would never have chosen from memory. Spoken language carries enough tells to categorize likelihood, never to convict. Small groups speaking in class add social pressure against cheating and hand the educator knobs on an incentive structure that today is a magical cheating genie.

Not foolproof. A student can build an agent told to seem dumb at first, then learn. That is fine. The goal is changing incentives, not certainty.

The care. Student voices identify them and can be cloned. Fingerprinting a voice is plausible technology; treat that as the reason for caution, not a feature. Newer offline models detect frustration and distress in speech with high confidence, and offline is what lets them meet zero-retention, no-sharing district rules on modest hardware. Any student-facing voice application gets maximum privacy, lowest risk, an accessible written alternative, and age-appropriate use only. A replay is context, never proof; define retention before collecting.

## 11. Grade learning, not the assistance log

27:00–29:00 · steady

<!-- image: two bar charts side by side, the first taller with a supporting brace, the second shorter and freestanding, an equals sign crossed out between them, dark slate background, amber accent on the brace, flat vector, no text -->

> Supported success is useful evidence
> Independent success is different
> A mismatch needs interpretation. Neither proves misconduct.

Bastani and colleagues found immediate supported performance and later unaided performance can diverge. Our response: record the conditions and include an independent check when that is the objective. Anxiety, difficulty, or forgetting can explain a mismatch. Give another opportunity; let a human interpret.

Source: Bastani et al. (2025), [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), PNAS. A [correction](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122) exists; check it before quoting figures.

## 12. Write the objective to the agent

29:00–32:00 · build

<!-- image: a whiteboard with three short handwritten lines and a small robot icon drawn in the corner as the addressee, a phone held up photographing the board, dark slate background, amber accent on the robot icon, flat vector, no text -->

> "You're my tutor. Don't give me answers. I'm here with three classmates."
> Photograph the whiteboard; the objective is the system prompt
> Push the practice run first; reward the student who asks for it unprompted

Seen in the wild: students telling a voice assistant not to give answers, then arguing about Lincoln with it for twenty minutes. Give it the learning objective by photographing the board. Prediction: we may end up writing objectives on the whiteboard addressed to the agent. "Tutor bot, help my students with..." The agent assesses to the rigor you set, explores stretch goals, fills remedial gaps, and shares what it saw with the teacher, aides, and parents as needed. Pre-program it with your objective-writing process; the 4Ms work.

The other half is discipline. At first, push it: want a practice run, flashcards, a pop quiz? Then reward the student who asks before the test unprompted. Let the agent name the connection distracted students miss. Hypothetical message, not data: "five flashcards an hour before produced a 98; your last three tests had no prep." Personalized recaps as a short audio file for the drive to school. A schedule that learns after band practice is the wrong time for chemistry. As someone with ADHD, an assistant that reminds me, reorders my day, and says "you did badly on the section quizzes after that reading, take a twenty-minute walk" is not a luxury. Applied cognitive science, balanced time, less cramming, and a channel toward wellbeing.

Story: [the student who told the tutor not to give answers]

## 13. AI collects evidence; people carry the workload

32:00–34:00 · steady

<!-- image: a robot arm placing highlighted paper excerpts into a tray, a person at the tray reading one excerpt with a cup of coffee, a clock on the wall, dark slate background, amber accent on the highlighted excerpt, flat vector, no text -->

> Draft follow-ups against a blueprint · Summarize with links to excerpts
> Flag uncertainty · Review by consequence · Measure the minutes

Bounded automation: draft questions from a teacher's blueprint, extract the sentence that suggests a misconception, prepare a summary where every claim links to its excerpt. If evidence is absent, say so. Sample outputs and track errors in both directions. Grading, accusations, and sensitive support stay human.

Then the workload. A richer assessment nobody has time to review fails operationally. Estimate minutes for prompts, calibration, sampling, exceptions. Repeated wrong bases across independent checks prompt support; a single corrected slip pages nobody.

Story: [a summary, human or machine, that invented a diagnosis the evidence did not support]

## 14. Widen the door

34:00–35:30 · warm

<!-- image: a lump of clay wrapped in cloth on a school desk beside a tablet showing a rough charcoal sketch on graph paper, a small bird perched on the desk edge, dark slate background, amber accent on the bird, flat vector, no text -->

> "I'm not carrying a brick of clay in my bag."
> "I'm no good with a pencil, but I like that look."
> Sketch → animate → 3D → game asset. Out of ideas? Ask.

I was told I was terrible at art. In my case it turned out true. For many kids it was disbelief, or supplies beyond standard issue. A friend found out after school they were a talented sculptor, later won awards, and once told me they never touched the wet clay in class. No car at home. Not carrying a brick of clay on the bus. That was it.

Those barriers reflect our socioeconomic structure and sit unexamined in the system. A model widens who gets to participate: the student who can't draw the charcoal graph-paper look can make it, then animate the birds at the lunch tables, then make it 3D, then a game asset. Ratchet up, lock in, stretch. When you run out of ideas, the model pre-programmed with your objectives will suggest the next one.

## 15. Redesign one checkpoint this week

> Permitted help → follow-up → rubric
> Access → retention → human review

Pairs, three minutes. Write the capability, the task, one purposeful follow-up, the permitted assistance, and the interpretation you could defend. Then your neighbor challenges one hidden assumption: does this also measure fluency, paid-tool access, or comfort speaking under pressure?

TEQSA's guidance supports contextual, multiple approaches. This worksheet is our implementation. Output: a reviewable sketch, then a small pilot.

## 16. A purpose-built machine

38:30–40:00 · land

<!-- image: a chessboard tilted so the pieces slide off, replaced by an open notebook with five short handwritten lines and a small precise instrument resting on the page, dark slate background, amber accent on the instrument, flat vector, no text -->

> Explain it. Challenge it. Change it. Apply it. Defend it.
> Too much power to hold casually. Prove the value constantly.

Stop trying to prove who typed the sentence. Ask whether the student can explain it, challenge it, change it, apply it, and defend it. Teaching has to change: how we include agents in the room, model their use, and design group work around them.

This is a great deal of data and a great deal of power. It is too much to hold casually. The value has to be proven, constantly, for educators and students, or the data should not exist. No ambling through conversations dispensing answers. A purpose-built machine. There are technical ways to build one; come find me after.

# The Future of Product Engineering

15 minutes · 9 slides · 15min

Timings are rehearsal targets, excluding Q&A. Speaker notes are delivery guidance, not a verbatim script.

## 1. The Future of Product Engineering

0.0–1.0 minutes

When the product team
becomes an executable loop

Open with a deliberately small product question: should onboarding ask a new user to invite teammates before they have seen any value? An AI can generate either screen quickly. The harder problem is deciding which screen deserves to exist and what evidence would change that decision. Promise a fifteen-minute argument about learning, taste, and ownership. This is a proposed operating model, not a claim that an autonomous product department is already solved. Keep returning to this onboarding choice as the concrete thread.

## 2. Code generation is the easy part to notice

1.0–2.5 minutes

A component can appear in seconds
The product question remains
What should exist?

Ask the audience to imagine receiving thirty polished onboarding variants before lunch. The backlog has not disappeared; it has moved into choosing, testing, and understanding consequences. A plausible interface can still solve the wrong problem. Shift attention from the speed of producing a component to the time between a customer signal and a decision supported by evidence. That is the larger opportunity in product engineering. Avoid claiming that code is universally cheap: integration, operation, and accessibility still require real engineering work.

## 3. The learning loop becomes programmable

2.5–4.5 minutes

Observe → understand
Hypothesize → experiment
Measure → judge
Learn → observe

Walk the loop using the onboarding story. Users visit integrations before invitations. A research step proposes that the order is confusing. A prototype tests a clearer first action. Instrumentation records a completed useful task, not just a click. A review considers support burden and user complaints. The result becomes a searchable decision for the next iteration. An agent can assist at several steps, but the arrows are responsibilities and evidence transfers, not proof that every step should execute without human involvement.

## 4. Research needs receipts

4.5–6.0 minutes

A support message is evidence
A cluster is an interpretation
A hypothesis is a proposal

Show three cards in your narration: a fictional support complaint, a cluster of similar complaints, and a proposed onboarding change. Keep their status distinct. One complaint does not estimate prevalence; a cluster can overrepresent vocal customers; a model's confident explanation does not establish cause. Require each synthesized claim to link to its evidence and preserve disconfirming cases. The practical benefit is inspectability: a product engineer can challenge the interpretation without reconstructing the entire conversation history or trusting an agent's summary on faith.

## 5. A win can be a worse product

6.0–9.0 minutes

Synthetic onboarding experiment
Activation: 40% → 48%
Support: 3% → 9%
Pressure copy uses false urgency

Run the compact version of the offline product demo. Ask whether an activation increase from forty to forty-eight percent sounds like a win. Then reveal the support rate rising from three to nine percent and the false urgency in the treatment. The fixture is synthetic and demonstrates a decision gate, not a live experiment or statistically established uplift. Give the audience a moment to change its answer. The system has optimized a visible metric while violating both an operational threshold and the product's promise.

| Candidate | Activation | Support | Urgency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control | 40% | 3% | None |
| Pressure copy | 48% | 9% | False |
| Clear first step | 45% | 4% | None |

## 6. Taste becomes a constraint on optimization

9.0–11.0 minutes

Support rate at most 5%
No fabricated urgency
No hidden exit
Human judgment still required

Show the product rules before revealing the decision. A support ceiling and a ban on fabricated urgency were chosen by humans in this fictional scenario; they are not universal standards. Some checks can be deterministic, such as whether an exit exists in the flow. Others need calibrated review, such as whether copy pressures a vulnerable user. A passed automated check grants eligibility for review, not permission to ship. Taste can become more explicit without pretending that every aspect of a good product fits a numeric score.

## 7. A quieter candidate survives review gates

11.0–12.5 minutes

Clearer first step: 45% activation
Support: 4%
No false urgency
Eligible for human review

Reveal the alternative treatment: a clearer first step, forty-five percent activation, and four percent support in the synthetic fixture. It passes the configured gates, but that does not prove it is the better product. A real experiment still needs trustworthy assignment, adequate observation, and a decision owner. The distinction is useful: automated evaluation can narrow the review queue without laundering an experiment into a business decision. Ask whether the team can explain why this candidate is eligible and why the other is blocked.

## 8. Humans own the product promise

12.5–14.0 minutes

Vision and strategy
Taste and consequential tradeoffs
Customer communication
Accountability for release

Place human ownership at the points where the organization makes commitments. A team can automate evidence gathering, draft hypotheses, and prepare reversible changes. It should explicitly name the people accountable for strategic direction, sensitive personalization, pricing, and release decisions. Those boundaries can change with risk and demonstrated reliability; they should not vanish because a system can technically perform an action. This is a design for shorter learning loops, not a story about removing people from every step or replacing judgment with dashboards.

## 9. Automate how the company learns

14.0–15.0 minutes

Keep the evidence
Encode the boundaries
Name the decision owner
Learn what deserves to exist

Return to the opening question about inviting teammates. The strongest artifact is now a decision with its evidence, limits, and owner, not merely a generated screen. Ask the audience to choose one existing handoff where useful evidence gets lost and make that handoff inspectable this week. Close with the user's signature idea: the future product engineer does not just automate building the product; they automate how the company learns what product to build. Pause after the final line and leave the loop on screen.

# Review: Stop Looking at My Benchmarks… Get Your Own!

Reviewed 2026-09-06 against the standard set by `outlines/free-tier-40min.md`.
Materials read: `flagship-talks/benchmarks-40min-outline.md`, `benchmarks-15min-outline.md`, `flagship-talks/README.md`, `reveal-talks/benchmarks.html`, plus Dan's posts `2026-05-06--llm-evals-are-broken` ("Fight Evils with Evals!") and `2026-08-11--auto-tune-your-llm-judge`.

---

## 1. Verdict

This would not win a competitive slot, and the reason is embarrassing: Dan already published a better version of it. "Auto-Tune Your LLM Judge" opens with a judge scoring 94, then 82, then 91, then 97, then 89, and names the thing — "noise wearing a lab coat." The talk's equivalent slide (11, "Calibrate the judge before trusting the score") is that post with the number, the metric, the joke and both arXiv citations removed. The single biggest problem is that the talk has no instrument: it tells eighteen slides' worth of true, sensible things about building an eval suite and never once asks whether the eval suite itself measures anything, which is the only question in this space that is actually hard and the only one that would make a program committee sit up. Everything else — the missing first-person story, the absent memorable number, thirty-two consecutive minutes with no audience moment — follows from that hole. The bones are good enough that a real rewrite is worth doing rather than a retirement.

## 2. The roast

**a) The only numbers in the talk are numbers you tell the room not to believe.**
Slide 2: "Model A · 89.7 / Model B · 88.9 / Model C · 84.3." Slide 3: "A · 71% pass · $0.34/run." The deck stamps both with the eyebrow "ILLUSTRATIVE · FICTIONAL DATA" and captions them "Invented teaching numbers. Sample size and latency are unspecified." So the audience's takeaway number is a number the speaker has personally disowned twice by minute five. Compare free-tier slide 10, which lands "dropping from seventy-five to forty-five percent acceptance costs you exactly the same as every token in your stack doubling in price overnight" — synthetic fixture, real arithmetic, checkable on the walk to the car. This talk has no such number. There is one sitting in plain sight on slide 18 and it is not picked up (see the missing discipline).

**b) Slide 4 apologizes to the thing in the title.**
Title: *Stop Looking at My Benchmarks… Get Your Own!* Slide 4, twenty seconds in: "Give public benchmarks their due… Their limitation is scope, not moral failure." Slide 1: "Public benchmarks still help with an initial shortlist." The title picks a fight and slide 4 shakes hands and buys the other guy a drink. Pick one. The generous read of the title is also unavailable, because "my benchmarks" implies Dan has benchmarks somebody is looking at — and there is no first person anywhere in either outline. The word "I" does not appear once in 18 slides.

**c) Slide 4 references a line that does not exist.**
Speaker note: "Explain the compressed-specification line." There is no compressed-specification line on slide 4, on the deck slide, or anywhere else in either outline. Either a good phrase was cut and the note survived it, or the note is instructing the presenter to explain something he was never given. Both are the same bug.

**d) The best sentence in the talk is not on the slide.**
Outline slide 5 is titled "One number is a comforting fiction." That is the one heading in the whole deck written in Dan's register. The rendered slide in `benchmarks.html` reads "Set constraints before choosing." — which is what a Jira ticket says. The deck also drops "the judge" from slide 11 ("Calibrate before trusting the score"), removing the subject of the sentence and leaving a slide that could be about anything.

**e) Thirty-two minutes with nobody in the room doing anything.**
Interaction beats, counted: slide 2, "invite a quick show of hands" (minute 1.5); slide 3, "Ask the audience to pick a model using only the public scores" (minute 3.5); slide 18, "Ask the room to choose one real workflow" (minute 37.5). That is it. Minutes 5.5 to 37.5 are twelve consecutive slides of three-bullets-and-guidance with no demo, no arithmetic on stage, no hands, no pairs. Free-tier at the same length has four audience moments and a live sensitivity table at its peak. Also, beats two and three are the same beat: slide 2's note already ran the show of hands on those exact three scores, so slide 3 re-asks a question the room answered ninety seconds ago.

**f) The peak is not a peak; it is slide eleven of eighteen, in the same font.**
By pacing, the climax should be judge calibration (slide 11, 20.5–23.5) — the moment where the talk turns on its own tooling. What it delivers: "Define a rubric; get expert labels / Inspect judge–expert disagreements / Version judge, prompt and rubric," and a note ending "disclose its limitations beside the score." Nothing about it is shaped differently from slide 16's release-gate hygiene. The talk has 18 slides and no crest.

**g) Two talks, and one of them belongs to a different talk in this portfolio.**
Slide 13: "Total operating cost ÷ successful tasks." Free-tier slide 10: "Cost per accepted outcome = total spend ÷ accepted jobs." Same arithmetic, same denominator argument, same "count the retries" caveat. Only one talk gets to own that, and free-tier owns it better because it runs a sweep table on stage. Slide 14 ("Routing is a system to evaluate") is a third talk again — it is the argument from `2026-01-02--llm-routing-mastra-ai`, compressed to four bullets. Cutting 13 and 14 costs the talk nothing it can't say in one sentence and buys back five minutes for the material that is missing.

**h) Speaker notes that summarize instead of speak, and the timing does not survive it.**
Each slide's note runs roughly 100–115 words. At a normal delivery rate that is fifty seconds of talking, allocated to a 2.0–3.0 minute slide. The notes are not underwritten scripts, they are abstracts: "A pile of traces becomes useful evaluation material only after somebody supplies an answerable question" is a summary of a thing to say, not a thing to say. Free-tier's notes are near-verbatim and time out honestly. Nobody can rehearse against this outline; they can only improvise against it for two-thirds of every slide.

**i) Disclaimer bloat, distributed rather than concentrated.**
Count the hedges: the "ILLUSTRATIVE · FICTIONAL DATA" eyebrow twice; "Do not identify these fictional labels with actual vendors" (2); "Say explicitly that these are invented teaching numbers" (3); "Even C is not automatically the production choice" (3); "Avoid inventing weights just to manufacture a single answer" (5); "Do not claim that a specific overall percentage follows from an unspecified workload mix" (12); "Clarify that twenty starts the conversation; it does not establish reliability" (18); the deck caption "Twenty is a starting point, not evidence of broad reliability" (18); plus the README's "Example data" paragraph. Nine. The free-tier standard is one, on slide 1, then argue. The cumulative effect here is a speaker who sounds like he is being deposed.

**j) The 30-minute adaptation deletes the argument and keeps the chores.**
README: "Benchmarks | Hide these slides: 4, 5, 6, 16, 17." The arithmetic works (five slides at 2.0 minutes each, 40 − 10 = 30). The content does not. Slides 4 and 5 are the entire case for why public benchmarks cannot answer your question; hiding them leaves a method with no motive. Worse, the transition instructs the presenter to "Introduce the cancellation job directly" — but slide 6 is where the support-assistant running example is established, and slide 6 is also hidden. The 30-minute version starts using a running example it never set up.

**k) Bluntly: the talk is weaker than Dan's own writing on the same subject, published four months earlier.**
"Fight Evils with Evals!" has "Every new model arrives wearing a tuxedo of benchmarks," the fake leaderboard joke "AGI: 127%!", and "You're using your users as test infrastructure. They didn't sign up for that." "Auto-Tune Your LLM Judge" has the 15-point spread cold open, the restaurant-bag line, a named metric he invented (*decision flip rate*), the observation that `78, 79, 81, 82, 80` looks stable and flips the verdict three times against an 80 threshold, position bias (arXiv 2406.07791), self-preference bias (arXiv 2410.21819), and OpenAI's and Anthropic's published grader guidance. The 40-minute talk contains exactly two lines with any voice in them — "otherwise the corpus becomes a museum" (17) — and the other one, "a transcript without an acceptance criterion is only a souvenir," appears only in the 15-minute version, so the flagship deck is the one with fewer jokes. Every asset above is already written, already sourced, already in his voice, and none of it made the stage.

## 3. The missing discipline

**Spine to steal: an eval suite is a measuring instrument, and nobody in this field validates theirs.** The talk currently argues "measure your own work," which is sensible and which the audience already agrees with by minute four. The thought-provoking version argues that the industry has reinvented psychometrics badly, that the field it reinvented has ninety years of results, and that most eval suites would fail the entry-level checks that a standardized test has to pass before anyone is allowed to score it.

**Goodhart and Campbell, in the original words.** Goodhart's actual 1975 formulation is not the bumper sticker: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes" (C.A.E. Goodhart, "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience," Reserve Bank of Australia conference paper, 1975; reprinted 1981). The famous "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" phrasing is Marilyn Strathern's, from "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system," *European Review* 5(3), 1997 — a paper about universities gaming assessment, which is a better story for this room than monetary policy. Campbell's law is sharper and rarely quoted correctly: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor" (Donald T. Campbell, "Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change," *Evaluation and Program Planning* 2(1), 1979). Campbell's examples were police clearance rates and standardized test scores. Both belong on the slide that currently reads "Benchmarks are somebody else's requirements," and they turn a mild observation into an indictment.

**Construct validity — the concept the talk is groping toward and never names.** Cronbach and Meehl, "Construct Validity in Psychological Tests," *Psychological Bulletin* 52(4), 1955, established the question: when your instrument produces a number, what construct is that a number *of*? Samuel Messick ("Validity," in *Educational Measurement*, 3rd ed., 1989) sharpened it further: validity is a property of the inferences you draw from a score, not a property of the test. That sentence alone reframes the whole talk — a benchmark is not valid or invalid, *your use of it* is. And this has already been applied directly to AI: Raji, Denton, Bender, Hanna and Paullada, "AI and the Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark," NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks, 2021, argues that general-capability benchmarks fail construct validity because the construct ("general reasoning") was never defined well enough to measure. That paper is the intellectual backbone this talk is missing, and it is on the same side of the argument as the title.

**Reliability before validity, and the arithmetic of small sets.** An instrument that gives different answers to the same input has no validity to discuss. Dan already knows this and wrote it up as *decision flip rate*; the talk should put that on stage. Then the number that fixes slide 18: **twenty cases, all passing, is consistent with a true failure rate of about fifteen percent.** That is the rule of three — with zero failures in *n* independent trials, the 95% upper bound on the failure rate is approximately 3/*n* (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, "If Nothing Goes Wrong, Is Everything All Right?," *JAMA* 249(13), 1983). Twenty for twenty means one in seven could still be broken and you would not have seen it. That is memorable, checkable on a napkin, and it makes the talk's own closing advice honest instead of merely modest. On statistical power in this exact field, Card, Henderson, Khandelwal, Jia, Mahowald and Jurafsky, "With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility," EMNLP 2020, found that a large share of published NLP comparisons are underpowered to detect the effects they claim.

**Inter-rater agreement, for the judge slide.** Cohen's kappa (Jacob Cohen, "A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales," *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 20(1), 1960) is the standard way to ask whether two graders agree beyond chance — and "two graders" here means your LLM judge and your expert. Krippendorff's alpha generalizes it to more raters and missing data. The point that earns its slide: raw agreement percentage is nearly useless, because a judge that passes everything agrees with your expert 90% of the time on a 90%-pass corpus while measuring nothing. **Flag as contested:** the familiar Landis and Koch (1977) verbal thresholds ("substantial," "almost perfect") are widely used and were explicitly described by their authors as arbitrary; kappa is also sensitive to prevalence, documented in Feinstein and Cicchetti, "High Agreement but Low Kappa," *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* 43(6), 1990. Say that out loud — admitting the metric has a known pathology is exactly the register that makes an audience trust the rest.

**Cranfield and TREC: the field that solved shared-task evaluation before anyone here was born.** Cyril Cleverdon's Cranfield experiments established test collections with fixed queries, fixed judgments and comparable runs ("The Cranfield Tests on Index Language Devices," *Aslib Proceedings* 19(6), 1967). NIST's TREC has run that paradigm annually since 1992 (Voorhees and Harman, eds., *TREC: Experiment and Evaluation in Information Retrieval*, MIT Press, 2005). They also hit every failure this room is about to hit and wrote it down: pooling bias and judgment incompleteness (Justin Zobel, "How Reliable Are the Results of Large-Scale Information Retrieval Experiments?," SIGIR 1998) and the durable finding that relative system rankings survive assessor disagreement even when absolute scores do not (Voorhees, "Variations in Relevance Judgments and the Measurement of Retrieval Effectiveness," *Information Processing & Management* 36(5), 2000). "A discipline solved your problem in 1967 and you are re-deriving it badly in a Notion doc" is a talk. "Protect the holdout" is a bullet.

**Contamination, for the shortlist argument.** The claim "the public score answers a different question" is weaker than the available claim: the public score may not answer any question, because the test set is in the training data. GPT-3's own paper contains a contamination analysis (Brown et al., "Language Models Are Few-Shot Learners," NeurIPS 2020, appendix on data contamination); Dodge et al., "Documenting Large Webtext Corpora," EMNLP 2021, found benchmark test data inside C4; Oren, Meister, Chatterji, Ladhak and Hashimoto, "Proving Test Set Contamination in Black Box Language Models," ICLR 2024, gives a statistical test that works without corpus access. Pair that with benchmark saturation — Kiela et al., "Dynabench: Rethinking Benchmarking in NLP," NAACL 2021, whose opening figure shows successive benchmarks reaching human-level performance faster and faster — and slide 4 stops being polite.

**Adaptive overfitting, for the holdout slide.** "If you repeatedly tune prompts against every available example, improvements may reflect familiarity with the set" is correct and has a formal treatment: Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold and Roth, "The Reusable Holdout: Preserving Validity in Adaptive Data Analysis," *Science* 349(6248), 2015. The empirical companion is Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt and Shankar, "Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?," ICML 2019 — new test sets built to match the original protocol, and accuracy dropped substantially across every model tested. **Verify the exact figures before quoting them**; the reported drops are large and vary by dataset and model, and the paper's own interpretation (distribution shift in the collection process, not classical overfitting) is the interesting part and is easy to misstate.

**The replication crisis as the parallel, used carefully.** Open Science Collaboration, "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science," *Science* 349(6251), 2015: 100 replications, a minority reproduced the original significant result. Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths" (*American Scientist* 102(6), 2014) explains how honest researchers reach false conclusions without ever cheating — which is precisely what a team does when it reruns an eval with a slightly different prompt until the number looks good. This is a one-slide analogy, not a section; the field earned its crisis over decades and the comparison gets glib fast.

**What to discard:** signal detection theory and ROC (real, relevant, but a second talk); Item Response Theory (genuinely applicable to benchmark item difficulty, and genuinely too much for a 40-minute product-engineering room); the cost-per-success accounting, which belongs to *Cry Me a Free Tier*.

## 5. The one thing

Put reliability on stage before minute twenty. Run the same judge on the same case five times, show the spread, name the decision flip rate, then do the rule-of-three arithmetic out loud: twenty green cases, one in seven could still be broken. Right now the talk asks the room to build an instrument and never asks whether the instrument works — which is exactly the mistake it accuses the benchmark leaderboards of making. Fixing that turns a sensible methods talk into an argument, gives the talk its missing number, and it costs Dan nothing to write because he already published it.

## 6. Facts to check or claims to soften

- **Rule of three (3/n):** correct for the 95% upper bound with zero observed events, but it assumes independent, identically distributed sampling from the population you care about. Eval cases hand-picked from incident reports are neither. Say the assumption on the slide; it makes the number stronger, not weaker.
- **Recht et al. 2019:** verify the exact accuracy drops and datasets before quoting a figure. The paper's own reading is distribution shift in test-set construction, not simple overfitting — misstating that will get a question from the back of the room.
- **Cohen's kappa thresholds:** Landis and Koch's verbal labels are conventional, not derived, and their authors said so. Kappa is prevalence-sensitive (Feinstein and Cicchetti 1990). Flag both rather than quoting a threshold as fact.
- **Goodhart attribution:** the popular phrasing is Strathern (1997), not Goodhart (1975). Getting this right in front of an economics-literate audience is free credibility; getting it wrong is the only thing they will remember.
- **Kiela et al. saturation figure:** it is a Dynabench framing figure, not a controlled longitudinal measurement. Describe it as an illustration of a trend, not a measured rate.
- **"Public benchmarks still help with an initial shortlist" (slide 1):** softening this is the wrong instinct — either defend it with the contamination caveat attached, or drop it. As written it concedes the title's fight before the fight starts.
- **Fictional data labelling:** keep the disclaimer, once, on slide 1, in the free-tier form ("every number in my fixture is invented"). Delete the other eight hedges, including both deck eyebrows and the README paragraph's redundancy.
- **README 30-minute table:** the Benchmarks row hides slide 6, which is where the running example is introduced, while its transition tells the presenter to use that running example. Fix or replace the row.
- **Deck/outline drift to reconcile before any delivery:** slide 5 heading ("One number is a comforting fiction" in the outline vs. "Set constraints before choosing" in the deck); slide 11 heading dropping "the judge"; slide 4's missing third bullet and its orphaned "compressed-specification line" note; slide 18's reordered bullets and the dropped "Expand coverage with evidence"; deck-only lines that never reached the outline, notably "A trace becomes a test when it answers a specific question" (8) and "Conceal identity. Check answer-order effects." (11), both of which are better than what the outline has.

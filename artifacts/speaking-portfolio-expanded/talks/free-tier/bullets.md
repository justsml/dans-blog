# Buy Me a Free Tier: bullet outline

15 slides, 40 minutes. [Canonical prose](index.md) · [Timing by route](timing.md). Six audience moments; no live tool.

## 1. Unlimited for $9.95

> August 2017: $9.95 monthly offer; $8.97 average US ticket.
> Don't fear training the model, worry how it's training you.

- August 2017.
- By June 2018, three million subscribers.
- Think about the incentive: another film costs the subscriber nothing.
- Don't fear training the model, worry how it's training you.

## 2. Four boxes, one invoice

> Price paid · Resources consumed · Cost allocated · Value delivered
> The gap between the last two is why nobody is measuring

- The invoice tells you what you paid.
- The useful gap for your own design review is between what you spent and what the customer accepted.
- When the invoice looks trivial, measuring that gap feels like more work than it saves.

Delivery: Show of hands, 15 seconds: which box can your dashboard answer?

## 3. Why would anyone sell below cost?

> Penetration pricing · Loss leader in a bundle · Predation · It is genuinely cheap
> The price cannot tell you which. What happens next can.

- Four explanations for a low price.
- The price alone cannot choose among them.
- The FTC's 2021 MoviePass complaint alleged password invalidation for the seventy-five thousand heaviest users, misleading fraud explanations, ticket verification and undisclosed caps.
- Customers who used the advertised allowance were responding to the offer.

## 4. Burn the boats

> A purchase commitment is not a margin disclosure.
> $250B incremental Azure services · Microsoft, 28 Oct 2025
> Credible commitment

- Microsoft's October 2025 announcement says OpenAI contracted for an incremental two hundred and fifty billion dollars of Azure services.
- Schelling's credible commitment names the strategic idea: making retreat costly can change what other players expect.
- Somebody is buying your free tier, and it is not you.

## 5. Free parking was never free

> The driver sees no meter, so demand looks infinite
> The cost moves into rents, prices, and land
> externality

- Shoup's free parking gives us externality: a cost borne outside the decision that creates it.
- The analogy has limits.
- In your system, start with recovery work.

## 6. Jevons, coal, and the extra lane

> 1865: less coal per unit of work; more coal used in total
> Add a lane, get traffic. Elasticity near one.
> Spend = price × jobs × calls per job × tokens per call

- Jevons wrote about coal in 1865.
- Induced demand is the related transport idea.
- For software, use the equation: spend equals price times jobs times calls per job times tokens per call.
- Epoch's March 2025 series, ending in February 2025, documents falling prices at fixed benchmark milestones.

## 7. Now do it to software

> Cheap call → easy approval → repeated default
> What did the extra call buy?

- A frontier call where a lookup would do.
- Each decision gets an immediate reward: less implementation work, a smoother demo, an easier sale.
- Those calls may be worth it.

Delivery: Show of hands, 15 seconds: who has shipped a frontier call where a lookup would do? Do not imply a personal admission without a verified example.

## 8. The lots are already built

> Repealing the parking minimum does not remove the asphalt
> path dependence
> The test: try to remove one model call and count the hours

- Path dependence means the route you took changes the routes still available.
- QWERTY is the famous example, and the evidence behind that story is disputed.
- Suppose a model writes a field that three downstream services now expect.

## 9. Whoever chooses is not whoever pays

> moral hazard
> The architecture is chosen in a design review. The bill arrives 60 days later, somewhere else.
> Shoup's fix was never a ban. It was a meter.

- Moral hazard describes how decisions change when the person choosing does not bear their costs.
- An engineer gets credit for shipping on Tuesday.
- A MoviePass subscriber using one movie a day was reading the offer.
- Put cost per accepted outcome next to the latency budget in the design review.

Delivery: Show of hands, 15 seconds: who sees per-feature cost without filing a ticket?

## 10. Doubling on a Tuesday

> Cost per accepted outcome = total spend ÷ accepted jobs
> Fixture: 2¢ inference + 1¢ other cost per attempt
> 3¢ ÷ 45% = 5¢ ÷ 75% = 6.67¢

- Define an accepted outcome before doing the division.
- Our fixture: a thousand attempts, two cents inference and one cent other cost each, seventy-five percent accepted.
- At forty-five percent acceptance, the same thirty dollars buys four hundred and fifty outcomes: six point six seven cents each.
- That equivalence belongs to this two-cent-plus-one-cent fixture.

Delivery: Thirty seconds: write one sentence defining an accepted outcome. Spend another 15 seconds writing the two divisions.

## 11. Turn the dial

> Sensitivity, not prediction.
> Multiply by your volume before you feel anything about it.

- Read the first and last rows: thirty dollars becomes two hundred and ten; four cents per accepted outcome becomes twenty-eight.
- Multiply by your volume before you feel anything about it.

Delivery: Walk the printed table for 15 seconds, then ask for 45 seconds: which assumption would you test before funding optimization?

## 12. Till Renewal Do Us Part

> Asset specificity. Hold-up is what it lets your counterparty do.
> An investment worth a lot inside this relationship and little outside it
> Your prompts, evals, fine-tunes, and that unlimited-usage clause

- Williamson called investments worth more inside one relationship than outside it asset specificity.
- Inventory your prompts tuned to one model, provider-specific evals, fine-tunes and latency assumptions.
- Your architecture is the collateral.

## 13. Reversible is a line item

> real option
> The option has value. It also has a premium: abstraction tax, eval upkeep, rehearsal time.
> Same project, different answer, depending on which world you are in

- A real option is a right you can exercise later.
- Take a router that halves inference while preserving acceptance.
- At a million attempts a month, that ten cents is a hundred thousand dollars.
- If building and operating the router costs more than it saves, keep the money.

## 14. Three prices for the next design review

> What we pay today · What we pay without the offer · What we can survive paying
> Record gross usage separately from credits
> Keep an acceptance suite · Rehearse one replacement · Price the portability

- Bring three prices to the next design review: what you pay today, what you pay without promotional treatment, and the highest price the product can survive.
- Record gross usage separately from credits.
- The third price needs a business answer: what is an accepted outcome worth, and how much of that value can inference consume?

Delivery: 45 seconds in pairs: identify the third price. No report-back inside this budget.

# Buy Me a Free Tier: 15-minute presenter script

Use slides 1, 3, 6, 7, 10, 12, 15. [Timing](timing.md) separates spoken delivery from interaction. Optional Story material replaces prose; it never extends the slot. Sources and visuals live in [the outline](index.md). This route teaches the price equation, incentives, acceptance denominator and asset specificity; the eight-term glossary is a handout, not a spoken promise.

## 00:00 to 02:15: slide 1, Unlimited for $9.95

On screen:

> August 2017: $9.95 monthly offer; $8.97 average US ticket.
> Don't fear training the model, worry how it's training you.

August 2017. MoviePass sells one movie a day for nine ninety-five a month. The average American ticket costs eight dollars and ninety-seven cents. MoviePass buys retail. Pay for one movie, see thirty.

Three million subscribers by June 2018, about ten months after launch. The service suspended in September 2019; the brand later returned with a different offer.

Don't fear training the model, worry how it's training you. Cheap decisions become defaults. That is an incentive analogy, not a measured habit study. Nobody outside a provider knows its margins; the workload dollars here are synthetic. MoviePass is a completed case, not a prediction about an AI vendor.

Delivery: Pause after the ticket arithmetic. The optional Story replaces prose within this budget; it is not extra time.

## 02:15 to 04:15: slide 3, Why would anyone sell below cost?

On screen:

> Penetration pricing · Loss leader in a bundle · Predation · It is genuinely cheap
> The price cannot tell you which. What happens next can.

Four explanations for a low price: buy adoption, support another paid product, remove rivals, or genuinely cheaper production. Predation has a legal test: below cost and a dangerous probability of recoupment. Price alone cannot tell you which explanation applies.

The FTC alleged MoviePass disrupted heavy users' passwords and imposed undisclosed caps. Settled without admission of liability. Watch access and renewal terms alongside price.

The enormous compute commitments everyone quotes are commitments, not margin disclosures. Somebody is buying your free tier, and it is not you. You are not a combatant. You are the terrain.

## 04:15 to 06:15: slide 6, Jevons, coal, and the extra lane

On screen:

> 1865: less coal per unit of work; more coal used in total
> Add a lane, get traffic. Elasticity near one.
> Spend = price × jobs × calls per job × tokens per call

Jevons described more efficient engines alongside more total coal consumption. Less coal per unit of work can enable more work. Backfire is possible, not a law.

Duranton and Turner found road travel growing roughly with highway capacity across US cities in 2011. That is induced demand on roads, not a measured token elasticity.

For your system: spend equals price times jobs times calls per job times tokens per call. Measure all four. A falling price need not produce a falling bill. It need not increase the bill either. The question is what value the changed consumption bought.

## 06:15 to 08:30: slide 7, Now do it to software

On screen:

> Cheap call → easy approval → repeated default
> What did the extra call buy?

A frontier call where a lookup would do. More context because it fits. Another retry because it is cheap. Unlimited usage promised against last month's tiny bill.

Each gets an immediate reward: less implementation work, a smoother demo, an easier sale. The promotional price becomes a curriculum; every design review is another rep.

Those calls may be worth it. Did the extra call buy more accepted outcomes? Without that measurement you have learned a habit without learning its price.

And removing the offer will not rewrite your defaults or your customer contracts. That is where a temporary price becomes lasting work.

Delivery: Show of hands, 15 seconds: who has shipped a frontier call where a lookup would do? Do not imply a personal admission without a verified example.

## 08:30 to 11:00: slide 10, Doubling on a Tuesday

On screen:

> Cost per accepted outcome = total spend ÷ accepted jobs
> Fixture: 2¢ inference + 1¢ other cost per attempt
> 3¢ ÷ 45% = 5¢ ÷ 75% = 6.67¢

Our synthetic fixture is a thousand attempts, two cents inference and one cent other cost each. At seventy-five percent accepted, thirty dollars buys seven hundred and fifty outcomes: four cents each.

Drop acceptance to forty-five percent: thirty dollars divided by four hundred and fifty is six point six seven cents. Double inference instead and keep acceptance at seventy-five percent: fifty dollars divided by seven hundred and fifty. Same answer.

That equality depends on this fixture's cost split. One change is on the front page. The other one is a Tuesday. Include recovery work when you define success.

Delivery: Thirty seconds: write one sentence defining an accepted outcome. Spend another 15 seconds writing the two divisions.

## 11:00 to 13:15: slide 12, Till Renewal Do Us Part

On screen:

> Asset specificity. Hold-up is what it lets your counterparty do.
> An investment worth a lot inside this relationship and little outside it
> Your prompts, evals, fine-tunes, and that unlimited-usage clause

Your prompts tuned to one model. Provider-specific evals and fine-tunes. A latency assumption. And an unlimited-usage promise to customers, priced against an input you cannot control. That sentence is the entire MoviePass balance sheet.

Williamson's asset specificity names investments worth more inside one relationship than outside it. Hold-up is the bargaining exposure after switching becomes expensive. Your architecture is the collateral.

Pick one model call and count the hours to remove it: output compatibility, evaluation, latency and recovery. Price that work against your exposure. Bring three rates to the review: today, without the offer, and the most you can survive.

## 13:15 to 15:00: slide 15, Cheap intelligence changes incentives first

On screen:

> Cost per accepted outcome
> Today · Without the offer · Highest survivable price
> The habit can outlive the offer.

MoviePass returned with a different offer. Theater operators also sell memberships. Owning the screens changes the economics of selling access. Unlimited access to someone else's retail input is another business.

The full talk's glossary stays in the handout. Keep this route's decision: measure cost per accepted outcome before deciding what to optimize. A worse result can erase a cheaper input.

Use the cheap inference. Measure what it rewards you for repeating. Keep the expensive decisions reversible while you find out.

The habit can outlive the offer.

Delivery: Stop talking. If questions follow, leave the acceptance arithmetic on slide 10 visible.

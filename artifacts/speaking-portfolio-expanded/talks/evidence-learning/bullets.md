## Slides

### 1. The cat is out of the bag

00:00–02:30 · warm · 02:30

> Horse out of the barn. Cat out of the bag.
> Math class survived the Casio watch.

- The historical parallels have been beaten to death, so one and done: the calculator panic.
- Confession: I may have used one or two on an assessment.
- Our calculator-clutching ancestors had to write a real trajectory into the curriculum.
- Story — The submission that made you realize the grade and the understanding had come apart.
- Do — Let the confession land before the pivot. The laugh is the title doing its job; do not explain the joke.

### 2. The doom loop

02:30–05:00 · warm · 02:30

> Proctoring fails. The pop quiz proves what you already knew.
> Getting away with it is the reward.
> Students are the original adversarial actors.

- It can feel like you've lost. The proctoring tools fail readily.
- Pummeling them with gotcha quizzes proves what you already knew: Johnny ain't learning too good.
- The detector is the same trap in software.
- Source — Liang et al. (2023), [GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779), Patterns.

### 3. Pencil first. Then the tool

05:00–07:00 · steady · 02:00

> Pencil, paper, whiteboard, peers: in the curriculum, with the reason
> Then, same document: "and we are going to teach you the tool"

- Write the trajectory down. Where does handwriting live, and why: the spatial and motor work a pencil activates, the argument you have out loud with a peer, the cohort you find by talking through a problem.
- Then, in the same document, tell students you're going to teach them the tool.
- This tension is older than the panic and it has a name.
- Source — Koedinger and Aleven (2007), [Exploring the Assistance Dilemma in Experiments with Cognitive Tutors](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0), Educational Psychology Review 19(3), 239 to 264.

### 4. The polished answer with a hole in it

07:00–09:00 · steady · 02:00

> "A 20% rise and a 20% fall cancel."
> The prose is clean. The denominator is wrong.

- Here's the fixture for the rest of the talk.
- The capability we're after is narrow enough for the room to inspect: explain why a percentage acts on a base, and how the base changes across a sequence.
- Do — Ask the room what the final answer alone would have told you. One answer, then move.

### 5. Outsmart move one: make verification feel like cheating

09:00–12:00 · build · 03:00

> "Take a picture of these hundred problems and make the model do them."
> A 1 that looks like a 7. A smudge that turns an 8 into a 9.
> "You didn't check the results? Johnny, you're not gonna make it."

- The highest-leverage assignment I know lets students feel like they're cheating.
- That one experience beats every ban, filter and lockdown.
- Privacy rides along for free. If you share Lake Tahoe, maybe not the photo of your grandparents' place on the water that any model can geolocate.
- Do — Thirty seconds: what would the smudge be in your subject? Take two.

### 6. Ration the help

12:00–14:00 · steady · 02:00

> "Which price does the discount use?" Learner: "The original $100."
> Clarify → conceptual hint → missing step → part of an example → full explanation → fresh reconstruction

- Two scripted turns. The diagnostic question finds where the base got lost.
- Then the ladder. Clarification: the second change acts on the new price.

### 7. Demo: four minutes inside a five-minute slide

14:00–19:00 · peak · 05:00

> $100 → $120 → $96. Why does the second base change?
> Now: $80 → +25% → −20%

- Run the scripted tutor turns — from the kit or read off the card — then go live.
- Then change the numbers: $80, up 25%, down 20%.
- Do — Runbook section 5 has the five scripted turns and the same table in print. If the room is quiet, present the prepared learner response; never pressure an individual into a public assessment. Two-minute compression: skip the kit, run only the $100 sequence, state the $80 item.

### 8. Score the reasoning. Write it down

19:00–22:30 · steady · 03:30

> Base identified 0–2 · Calculation justified 0–2 · Transfer explained 0–2 · Help in its own column
> Record: concept · what they said · what help they got · next check
> Never "weak at reasoning." Never "likely cheated."

- Zero: the wrong base persists. One: the right number, no explanation.
- The record is four lines. Concept: changing percentage base.
- Bastani and colleagues found supported performance and later unaided performance can diverge, and the safeguards that matter come from teachers.
- And that finding is not news, which is the useful part.
- Source — Bastani et al. (2025), [Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), PNAS. The [correction](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122) (August 2025) fixes an author affiliation; no result changed.
- Source — Soderstrom and Bjork (2015), [Learning versus performance: an integrative review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000), Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(2), 176 to 199.

### 9. Outsmart move two: make them say it out loud

22:30–25:30 · build · 03:00

> Recall errors and reading errors are different animals
> Small groups, speaking: the incentives reset
> Voice is biometric. Offline. Zero retention. Age-appropriate. Written alternative.

- Answering from memory and reading a model's output aloud produce different mistakes.
- Small groups, speaking, in class: that resets the table.
- Now the care, and it's real. A student's voice identifies them and can be cloned.

### 10. Outsmart move three: read all 300 transcripts

25:30–28:00 · build · 02:30

> Teacher talk · student talk · think time · AI time
> Guided by you, or alone with the model?
> "Show me five students who took a path worth showing the room."

- You already count who's talking, how much silence there is, whose engagement it is.
- Then the view you have never had. Every transcript from a class of 30 or a course of 300, and a qualitative pass run by the model.
- And know what you are up against when you read the model's summary instead of the transcript.
- Story — A transcript that showed you a path to the answer you would not have taught.
- Source — Skitka, Mosier and Burdick (1999), [Does automation bias decision-making?](https://doi.org/10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252), International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 51(5), 991 to 1006.

### 11. Write the objective to the agent

28:00–31:00 · build · 03:00

> "You're my tutor. Don't give me answers. I'm here with three classmates."
> Photograph the whiteboard; the objective is the system prompt
> Push the practice run first. Then reward the kid who asks for it.

- Seen in the wild: students telling a voice assistant not to give them answers, then arguing about Lincoln with it for twenty minutes.
- The other half is discipline. At first, the agent pushes: want a practice run, flashcards, a pop quiz?
- Story — The student who told the tutor not to give answers.

### 12. Widen the door

31:00–33:30 · warm · 02:30

> "I'm not carrying a brick of clay in my bag."
> "I'm no good with a pencil, but I like that look."
> Sketch → animate → 3D → game asset. Out of ideas? Ask.

- I was told I was terrible at art. In my case it turned out to be true.
- Those barriers reflect our socioeconomic structure and sit unexamined in the system.

### 13. Redesign one checkpoint this week

33:30–37:30 · peak · 04:00

> Capability → task → the smudge → follow-up → permitted help → rubric
> Minutes to review a class set
> Neighbor challenges one hidden assumption

- Pairs, three minutes. Write the capability, the task, the smudge that makes verification feel like cheating, one purposeful follow-up, the help you'll permit, and the interpretation you could defend to a parent.
- Then your neighbor challenges one hidden assumption: does this also measure fluency, paid-tool access, or comfort speaking under pressure?
- Do — Three minutes writing, one minute of challenge. Call time on the challenge, not the writing.
- Source — TEQSA (2023), [Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligence](https://www.teqsa.gov.au/guides-resources/resources/corporate-publications/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence).

### 14. A purpose-built machine

37:30–40:00 · land · 02:30

> Explain it. Challenge it. Change it. Apply it. Defend it.
> Too much power to hold casually. Prove the value constantly.

- Stop trying to prove who typed the sentence.
- The kid next to the Casio owner didn't need a watch.
- This is a great deal of data and a great deal of power.
- Do — Land on "come find me after." Stop.

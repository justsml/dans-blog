Educators hear two promises: every student gets a private genius, or every assignment is now worthless. Neither tells you what to do on Monday.

Skepticism as a method

  1. What is the claim?
  2. What would count as evidence?
  3. Who benefits if we believe it?

A claim that can fail: students explain source bias on a new document next week.

2:30–5:00 · warm

> What is the claim?
> What would count as evidence?
> Who benefits if we believe it?

Skepticism should produce better questions, not permanent refusal. A vendor says the tutor improves learning: ask what students did unaided afterward. A colleague says the tool destroys thinking: ask which task, which learners, which help. Both need a comparison. Write the claim so it could fail: "students using this hint sequence will explain source bias on a new document next week."

Two failures you will hear about this year: the confident wrong answer that got an A, and the pilot that improved scores and nothing else. Both are design failures, and both can be anticipated.

17:00–21:00 · build

> Bastani: assisted practice up, unaided performance down, safeguards helped
> Kestin: a designed tutor beat active learning on immediate outcomes
> Which learners, which teaching moves, which comparison, how long after

Bastani and colleagues ran a high-school mathematics trial. Unrestricted GPT access improved practice and worsened later unaided performance; a version with teacher-designed safeguards mitigated the harm. One intervention, one setting. Reuse the design lesson, not a headline percentage, and check the corrected article before quoting numbers.

Kestin and colleagues found a carefully engineered AI tutor outperformed the active-learning comparison in a university physics course on immediate outcomes. Keep the treatment attached to the finding.

Together the two studies should make you curious about instructional design rather than eager to pick a winner. Ask what the tutor makes the learner do: attempt, expose an assumption, check a new example. Handling: record supported and independent performance separately. A dashboard counting completed exercises tells you the system is busy.

Sources: Bastani et al. (2025), Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning, PNAS, with a correction · Kestin et al. (2025), AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning, Scientific Reports.

≠ learning evidence
≠ misconduct decision

Liang et al. found fairness and robustness problems in the detectors they tested.

GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers

21:00–23:00 · build

> Authorship inference ≠ learning evidence ≠ misconduct decision

Liang and colleagues found the detectors they tested produced false positives on nonnative English writing and were sensitive to rewriting. Evidence about tested tools, not every future detector. Even a good authorship signal would not tell you whether the student can weigh sources. Handling: no disciplinary consequence is automated from a score. And no detector stunt on stage.

Source: Liang et al. (2023), GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers, Patterns.

Pairs · 2 minutes · confirmed error, vanished gains, false accusation, unequal access

25:00–29:00 · peak

> What happened?
> What is the smallest change that would have caught it?

Pairs, two minutes. One partner names how the assignment's AI policy failed: confirmed error, vanished gains, false accusation, unequal access, or something new. The other names the smallest change that would have caught it. Then thirty seconds of shared answers.

This is how confidence is earned. Not by trusting the tool, but by having already imagined its failure and put a check there.

  2. Right answer, faulty reasoning
  3. Request for the solution

Proportional evidence

“Which source did you trust more, and what would change your mind?”

Two educators score the teaching moves. Count factual errors separately.

29:00–32:30 · steady

> Eval set: a confident wrong answer, a right answer with faulty reasoning, a request for the solution, a learner the tutor misunderstands
> Evidence: one added question, a changed constraint, a correction with a reason, a later independent check

Four cases before any pilot. For the history example, the tutor must not affirm "older means more reliable." Two educators score the teaching moves; factual errors counted separately from pacing and tone. A benchmark score cannot certify your lesson.

Then proportional evidence. Add one question that improves the inference: "which source did you trust more, and what would change your mind?" Collect enough to support a decision, not enough to reconstruct a student's life.

AI scales instruction far more easily than it scales wisdom. A tutor supplies another exercise fast. A teacher notices the student reasons well aloud but freezes on the written form, or that a language issue is being scored as a history error. Reserve human attention for interpretation and consequential grading. A human escalation promise needs an actual route.

Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligence

34:30–38:00 · land

> Name the capability · Specify permitted assistance
> Compare evidence before and after · Decide whether to keep it
> To the vendor: what learning improved, compared with what, for whom, at what staff cost?

Redesign one assignment, not the institution. Write the objective and a simple rubric. Keep a baseline sample. Estimate staff time. Agree what improvement justifies continuing. Invite students to report confusing instructions. Two educators grade a sample and resolve disagreements first. TEQSA's guidance supports multiple contextual approaches; this is one local implementation.

In the next vendor demo, ask them to walk through one learner misconception and show the teaching response. Ask what was measured after assistance stopped, what happens when the tutor is wrong, and what work falls back onto staff. A tool that saves preparation time without improving learning may still be worth buying if it is sold that way.

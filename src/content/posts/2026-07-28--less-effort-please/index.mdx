AI products have trained us to turn every knob clockwise.

More context. More tools. More tokens. More thinking.

<blockquote class="inset">
  Apparently intelligence is a guitar amp. Let's turn it up to 11!
</blockquote>

So I tested the knob.

While tuning [ExploitHunter.app](https://github.com/justsml/ExploitHunter.app), I ran GPT-5.6 Sol through the same browser-based XSS hunt at every reasoning level it supports: `none`, `low`, `medium`, `high`, and `xhigh`. Same target. Same tools. Same judge. Three runs per level.

I expected a staircase. Each step up would buy a little quality and cost a little time.

The staircase was built by M.C. Escher.

## Same model, same hunt, five personalities

Read these as route-level results. A refusal counts as a failed run, because in production a refusal does not become a success just because a spreadsheet politely excluded it.

| Thinking | Answers returned | Judge | Runtime | Cost | Tool calls |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `none` | 3/3 | **100%** | 278s | $0.316 | 11.0 |
| `low` | 2/3 | 64.2% | 103s | **$0.144** | **3.3** |
| `medium` | 2/3 | 65.3% | **91s** | $0.177 | 4.3 |
| `high` | 2/2 | 97.9% | 99s | $0.169 | 4.0 |
| `xhigh` | 3/3 | 95.5% | 92s | $0.172 | 4.3 |

The judge column is the mean across every run at that level, with refusals scored as failures. Runtime, cost, and tool calls are per-run averages. The `high` row only has two clean runs, so treat it as promising rather than canonical.

Yes, `none` got full marks.

It also took three times as long as `xhigh`, made two and a half times as many tool calls, and burned nearly three times the tokens.

We did not remove the thinking. We moved it into the tool loop and paid retail for it.

<p class="inset">
Turn thinking off and the model starts thinking out loud with your infrastructure.
</p>

## "None" is not nothing

With no internal reasoning, Sol still found its way to strong answers. It just wandered there: browser action, observation, correction, repeat. Eleven tool calls to do what `xhigh` did in four.

The route was accurate. It was also operationally absurd.

Token accounting misses this. Reasoning tokens have a visible price, so they look like the expensive part. Hesitation hides in tool calls, screenshots, retries, a growing context window, and wall-clock time. Waste becomes sophisticated once it has several billing columns.

`low` and `medium` found a different optimization: refuse one run in three. The answers they did return were fine. The route succeeded two-thirds of the time. An omitted result is wonderfully cheap right up until somebody wanted it.

`high` and `xhigh` occupied the useful middle. Roughly the same runtime, cost, and tool count, with strong answers on every run that completed. `high` edged `xhigh` on quality, but with two runs that is a hint, not a canonization.

There is no monotonic ladder here. There is a behavioral switchboard.

## Optimize indecision

Thinking effort is not a quality slider. It changes where the model searches, how often it acts, whether it refuses, and when it decides the job is done.

So stop scoring the answer and start scoring the route:

- Did it answer at all?
- Was the answer good?
- How long did the investigation take?
- What did it spend while visibly "not thinking"?

By those measures, the cheapest reasoning setting was not the cheapest reliable agent. The highest setting was not the highest-scoring one. And `none` was less an absence of thought than an expensive public performance of it.

Do not minimize reasoning tokens. Minimize total indecision.

Less effort, please.
